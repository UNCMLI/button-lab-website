Research Areas

Investigating the fundamental mechanisms of airway mucus clearance and its dysfunction in disease.

Research Area 1

Cystic Fibrosis & Mucus Dysfunction

Airway mucus clearance depends on the coordinated function of the periciliary layer (PCL) and the overlaying mucus gel. The PCL, a water-rich layer directly adjacent to the epithelium, lubricates ciliary beating; the mucus layer traps particles and is transported by cilia. When mucus becomes hyperconcentrated—too thick and dehydrated—it adheres to the airway surface and clearance fails.

Our work has established mucus hyperconcentration as a unifying mechanism in chronic bronchitis, cystic fibrosis, and bronchiectasis, explaining why structurally different diseases share a common physiological phenotype.

Key Biological Questions

  • What determines optimal mucus hydration at the molecular level?
  • How does mucus concentration regulate osmotic pressure and transport?
  • Can we restore normal clearance by correcting mucus concentration?
  • What role do submucosal glands play in CF airway dysfunction?
  • How do mucus plugs form, and what determines whether they dissolve or persist?

Approach

We combine in vitro air-liquid interface cultures, mouse models, and human clinical studies to measure mucus concentration, osmotic pressure, and transport rates. Our landmark 2012 Science paper identified the periciliary brush—a proteoglycan-rich matrix that physically separates mucus from the epithelial surface—as essential for maintaining a distinct PCL.

Clinical Relevance

Understanding mucus hyperconcentration has directly informed hydration-based therapeutic strategies now used in CF treatment, including hypertonic saline therapy and CFTR modulators.

Research Area 2

Airway Surface Liquid Regulation

The airway surface liquid (ASL) is a thin fluid layer that bathes the respiratory epithelium. Its volume and composition determine ciliary beat frequency, mucus hydration, and the efficiency of mucociliary transport. ASL volume is regulated by a balance of ion transport (especially CFTR-mediated chloride secretion and ENaC-mediated sodium absorption), osmotic forces, and mechanical shear stress.

We discovered that mechanical forces—breathing, coughing—trigger ATP release from airway epithelia, which activates purinergic receptors and drives transient ASL secretion. This mechanochemical feedback loop helps maintain hydration during variable physiological conditions.

Key Biological Questions

  • How do cells sense and respond to mechanical stress?
  • What role does ATP/adenosine signaling play in ASL volume regulation?
  • How is ASL homeostasis disrupted in cystic fibrosis?
  • What are the critical thresholds for ASL volume supporting normal clearance?
  • How do environmental stressors — warming climates, dry inhaled air, and heat — challenge ASL homeostasis?

Approach

Our work combines electrophysiology, live-cell imaging, and mathematical modeling to characterize the feedback circuits that regulate ASL. We demonstrated that CFTR-dependent anion secretion is required for maintaining ASL volume under basal conditions and that defective CFTR leads to ASL dehydration via increased ENaC activity.

Therapeutic Implications

These findings have guided the development of ion transport modulators and osmotic agents designed to restore ASL volume in muco-obstructive diseases.

Research Area 3

Mucociliary Clearance Mechanics

Cough is the lungs' backup clearance mechanism when ciliary transport fails. We discovered that cough clearance depends critically on the adhesive and cohesive forces within the mucus layer. Highly concentrated, viscous mucus adheres strongly to the airway surface (increased adhesion) and to itself (increased cohesion), resisting removal by airflow.

Our peel force measurements revealed that CF and chronic bronchitis mucus exhibits dramatically elevated adhesive and cohesive forces compared to normal mucus. This mechanical abnormality explains why patients with thick mucus cannot clear secretions even with vigorous coughing.

We recently identified a third mechanism of mucus clearance: cilia-independent gas-liquid transport (GLT), in which airflow during tidal breathing propels mucus along the airway surface even when cilia cannot. Using in vitro airway cultures and cilia-deficient mice, we demonstrated that GLT occurs at tidal-breathing flow rates, depends critically on mucus concentration, and is modulated by breathing frequency and body orientation — establishing GLT as a clinically significant clearance pathway in ciliary dysfunction and pointing to exercise-, hydration-, and mucolytic-based strategies to restore clearance in these patients.

Key Biological Questions

  • What molecular properties of mucins determine adhesion and cohesion?
  • How do we quantify these mechanical properties in patient samples?
  • Can we reduce adhesion/cohesion therapeutically without disrupting normal function?
  • What is the relationship between mucus rheology and clearance efficiency?

Approach

We developed novel microforce balance techniques to measure mucus peel forces from airway surfaces. These methods enable direct mechanical characterization of mucus from patients and animal models, providing quantitative endpoints for therapeutic testing.

Impact

This work reframes therapeutic targets: instead of merely thinning mucus, we should focus on reducing adhesion and cohesion to enable effective cough clearance.

Research Area 4

Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia & Ciliopathy Biology

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a genetically heterogeneous disorder in which defective motile cilia compromise mucociliary clearance from birth. We study how ciliary dysfunction interacts with mucus concentration, airway geometry, and gravity to determine whether mucus remains mobile, forms adherent plaques, or progresses to airway-occluding plugs.

Our recent identification of cilia-independent gas-liquid transport during tidal breathing has expanded this framework: airflow can transport sufficiently hydrated mucus even when ciliary beating is absent. We are defining the material and physiological conditions that permit this residual clearance pathway, and how hydration, breathing, and mucolytic strategies might be combined to improve airway clearance in PCD and related ciliopathies.

Key Biological Questions

  • How do ciliary motility defects alter mucus transport, retention, and regional plug formation?
  • What mucus concentration and mechanical thresholds permit gas-liquid transport when cilia fail?
  • How do breathing pattern, body orientation, cough, and airway hydration interact to support clearance in PCD?
  • Which chemical or physical interventions restore functionally meaningful clearance in cilia-impaired airways?

Approach

We combine differentiated human airway cultures, quantitative ciliary-beat and mucus-transport measurements, cilia-deficient mouse models, and gravity-controlled imaging platforms. These systems allow us to resolve cilia-dependent and cilia-independent contributions to clearance and to test intervention strategies with direct functional endpoints.

Translational Goal

By identifying clearance mechanisms that remain available when motile cilia are impaired, we aim to develop practical strategies that reduce mucus retention and progressive airway injury in PCD.

Research Area 5

Mucus Plug Pathogenesis & Clearance

Mucus plugs are hyperconcentrated mucus masses that obstruct airways and are increasingly recognized as active drivers of airway dysfunction, not merely residual secretions. Our current work tests the obstruction paradigm: plug osmotic pressure can compress the periciliary layer, stall clearance, and impose sustained mechanical loads on the airway wall that may contribute to distension, epithelial remodeling, and progression toward bronchiectasis.

We also define why plugs persist and how they can be cleared. Static aqueous exposure may soften a plug without producing meaningful dissolution, because transport into and through the dense mucin network is diffusion-limited. We therefore test chemical mucolytics and mucinases together with mechanical adjuncts, including cyclic compression and ultrasound, and pair dissolution with sustained hydration to prevent re-concentration after the plug opens.

Key Biological Questions

  • How does mucus concentration generate the osmotic forces that remodel airway walls and submucosal glands?
  • What distinguishes a reversible mucus gel from a persistent, airway-occluding plug?
  • Which chemical and mechanical interventions accelerate dissolution and produce mucus that can be cleared by cilia or cough?
  • Does clearing plugs restore regional airway function and reverse early airway-wall remodeling?

Approach

We integrate human mucus plugs and airway cultures with quantitative dissolution assays, force measurements, mouse models of CF-like and asthma-like mucus obstruction, and a cough-competent sheep occlusion model. Across these systems, we measure plug material properties, proximal-versus-distal clearance, delivery mode, and airway-wall recovery after clearance.

Therapeutic Principle

Effective plug therapy must do more than reduce viscosity: it must overcome the local dissolution barrier, reduce adhesion and cohesion, and maintain hydration long enough for the mobilized mucus to clear.

Research Area 6

Therapeutic Approaches

Restoring mucus hydration is a rational therapeutic strategy for muco-obstructive diseases. Our work has evaluated multiple approaches: hypertonic saline, TMEM16A potentiators, CFTR modulators, and osmolyte-based strategies.

We demonstrated that nebulized hypertonic saline improves mucociliary clearance in chronic bronchitis and that even partial restoration of CFTR function (~25% of normal) can normalize mucus transport rates in CF airway epithelia.

Key Biological Questions

  • What level of CFTR function is required to normalize clearance?
  • Which therapeutic approach is most effective for which patient subgroup?
  • Can we combine approaches for synergistic benefit?
  • How do we overcome mucus adhesion/cohesion barriers to therapy?

Approach

We conduct clinical trials in CF and chronic bronchitis patients, measure clearance using scintigraphy and nasal dye clearance, and use in vitro systems to screen novel compounds.

Clinical Translation

Our research has informed multiple therapies now in clinical use, including hypertonic saline, mannitol, and CFTR modulator combinations.

Research Area 7

Dry Air & Airway Health

Inhaled air is not merely a carrier of oxygen — its water content directly shapes airway health. As global temperatures rise, the air humans breathe has an ever-greater drying potential, and our recent work shows that this dehydrating air poses a tangible threat to the lung's mucus barrier. When the inspired airstream is too dry, evaporation during tidal breathing progressively thins the airway mucus layer and compresses the underlying epithelium, disrupting the carefully balanced hydration on which mucociliary clearance depends.

In a 2025 study in Communications Earth & Environment, we combined continuum modeling with well-differentiated human tracheal-bronchial cultures to show that breathing progressively drier air (95%, 60%, and 30% relative humidity at body temperature) thins the mucus layer by 5%, 35%, and 58%, respectively. The associated compression of airway epithelial cells elevates secretion of inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-33, and IL-6, and mice with a muco-inflammatory phenotype exposed to intermittent dry air for one week develop measurable pathological changes. Coupled with climate model simulations, this work predicts that large parts of the United States will face elevated airway-inflammation risk by the latter half of this century.

We have extended this line of inquiry to heat waves, which increasingly expose airways — often during mouth breathing — to extreme dryness. Using airway-lining interface cultures of human bronchial epithelial cells, we found that the mucosal collapse caused by dry heat alters expression of genes central to airway hypersensitivity and ion transport, including TRPV4, PIEZO1, SCNN1A, SCNN1B, ANO1, and CFTR. Critically, this collapse can be reversed: inhaled alkaline (pH > 8) hypertonic divalent-salt aerosols rehydrated the epithelial surface, and reanalysis of cough recordings from a recent clinical trial showed such aerosols reduced placebo-adjusted cough bouts by 34% among refractory chronic cough patients. This work, reported in Scientific Reports (in press), positions inhaled endogenous ions as a potential buffer against the growing respiratory burden of a warming climate.

Key Biological Questions

  • How does the drying potential of inhaled air disrupt mucus layer hydration?
  • Which molecular pathways link dehydration-driven epithelial compression to inflammation and airway hypersensitivity?
  • What regional and population thresholds determine vulnerability to dry-air injury?
  • Can targeted strategies — from reconditioning inspired air to inhaled ion therapies — protect the airway barrier?

Approach

We integrate mathematical modeling of water transport in the airway surface liquid, climate-model projections, physiology in well-differentiated human airway cultures and mouse models with muco-inflammatory phenotypes, and clinical data on inhaled therapeutic aerosols. This lets us connect planetary-scale changes in atmospheric water content to molecular events at the epithelial surface.

Implications

Understanding how dry air drives mucus dehydration, inflammation, and cough hypersensitivity points toward both physiological damage-mitigation strategies and practical interventions — from humidifying and conditioning inspired air to aerosol therapies — that preserve mucus barrier function as the climate continues to warm.

Research Area 8

Computational & Mathematical Models

Quantitative understanding requires mathematical models that integrate ion transport, fluid dynamics, and mechanical forces. We developed mechanochemical models of ASL regulation that predicted behaviors not apparent from single experiments—for example, that the system exhibits bistability near critical thresholds of CFTR function.

These models have been validated in cell culture and mouse models, and they now guide therapeutic hypothesis generation and patient stratification.

Key Biological Questions

  • What are the critical thresholds of CFTR function for normal clearance?
  • Can we predict which patients will respond to specific therapies?
  • How do multiple interventions interact when combined?
  • What are the system's emergent properties not apparent from components alone?

Approach

Our models incorporate ATP release kinetics, purinergic receptor activation, ion channel dynamics, and osmotic water flow. We use both analytical solutions and numerical simulation to explore parameter space and generate testable predictions.

Value of Modeling

Mathematical models enable us to test thousands of parameter combinations computationally, identifying optimal therapeutic strategies before costly clinical trials.

Landmark Discovery

The Periciliary Brush Model

Previously, the periciliary layer was thought to be simple fluid. In our landmark 2012 Science paper, we discovered that it contains a structured brush of mucins that physically separates mucus from the epithelial surface. This brush is essential for ciliary function and explains why mucus and cilia can coexist without fouling.

When mucus hyperconcentrates, the brush collapses, mucus contacts the epithelium directly, and clearance fails. This finding fundamentally changed how the field conceptualizes mucociliary clearance.

Scientific Impact

  • Explains how the PCL maintains distinct properties from overlying mucus
  • Reveals why mucus dehydration leads to clearance failure
  • Guides development of therapies targeting the periciliary space
  • Provides a framework for understanding ciliary dysfunction in disease

Our Framework

Scientific Philosophy

Biophysical Framework for Airway Physiology

Our research is unified by a biophysical perspective on airway physiology. We view the airways not merely as a collection of cells and molecules, but as a mechanical system governed by physical forces—osmotic pressure gradients, adhesive energies, fluid shear stress, and interfacial tension.

This framework emerged inductively from our experimental observations: mucus transport cannot be understood without quantifying the forces that mucus exerts on surfaces, and ASL volume cannot be predicted without modeling the osmotic balance between cells, fluid, and the extracellular matrix.
