# Illustration Prompt 01: Cystic Fibrosis & Mucus Dysfunction

## Intended website section

Research Area 1: Cystic Fibrosis & Mucus Dysfunction

## Scientific story to communicate

Show the transition from a hydrated, transportable mucus gel above a protected periciliary layer to hyperconcentrated mucus that osmotically compresses the periciliary brush, contacts the epithelial surface, and stalls clearance. This is a biophysical story about concentration and osmotic pressure, not a generic "thick mucus" image.

## Reference concepts from Button Lab work

- Button et al., Science 2012: structured periciliary brush separates mucus from airway epithelium.
- Hill et al., Eur Respir J 2018: pathological CF mucus and clearance failure result from increased concentration, not altered pH.
- Kato et al., Science Advances 2022: concentration-dependent abnormalities unify superficial-airway and submucosal-gland dysfunction in CF.
- Hill et al., Physiological Reviews 2022: airway mucus physiology and pathophysiology.

## Primary generation prompt

Create a premium biomedical editorial illustration, horizontal 16:9, showing a single conducting-airway cross section in a clear left-to-right transition from healthy airway surface defense to cystic-fibrosis mucus dysfunction. On the left, show a smooth hydrated teal mucus gel riding above a distinct, pale-blue periciliary layer populated with upright fine brush-like glycocalyx filaments and coordinated cilia; tiny neutral particles are trapped in the upper mucus and carried in one direction. On the right, show the same airway under CF-like fluid depletion: a denser darker teal-blue mucus gel with closer mucin mesh spacing, visibly reduced water space, compressed and bent periciliary brush, shortened cilia trapped under the gel, and mucus contacting the epithelial surface. Make the dense gel feel osmotically loaded through gentle downward compression arrows and a subtle inward pressure gradient, but use no numbers or written labels. Include a small submucosal gland duct feeding into the airway wall, with impaired outflow on the diseased side. Keep the epithelial cells anatomically recognizable and the lumen open enough to read the comparison. Use elegant flat-to-soft 2.5D vector rendering, deep UNC navy outlines, Carolina blue and teal for hydration states, restrained muted coral only at the compressed diseased interface, warm off-white background, high scientific precision, ample clean negative space in the upper right for website copy. No text, no logos, no labels, no photorealism.

## Optional tighter alternative

Create a two-panel longitudinal airway-surface schematic, healthy hydrated mucus on the left and hyperconcentrated cystic-fibrosis mucus on the right, centered on the gel-on-brush model. The critical visual contrast is the tall hydrated periciliary brush and freely beating cilia on the left versus the collapsed brush, compressed cilia, and surface-adherent dense mucus gel on the right. Use the same airway epithelial geometry in both panels so concentration, not anatomy, is visibly causal.

## Negative prompt

Do not depict alveoli, blood clots, pus, a smoking-related COPD lung, a generic chest X-ray, a human patient, DNA helices floating in the lumen, exaggerated green slime, text, labels, logos, or an entirely occluded airway. Do not imply that acidic pH is the central lesion.

## Art-direction checklist

- Disease side must be denser by mesh spacing and reduced water space, not just darker.
- Periciliary brush must remain visually distinct from the mucus gel.
- Cilia should be compressed by the gel, not absent.
- The image should read as conducting-airway surface biology within two seconds.
